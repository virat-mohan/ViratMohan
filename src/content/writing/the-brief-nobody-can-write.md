title: "The Brief Nobody Can Write"
standfirst: "A hundred F&B brands taught me the hardest part of marketing was never the campaign. It was that nobody could tell you, in one place, what was actually true about the brand."
order: 4
pubDate: 2026-08-03
---

Somewhere around the fortieth brand, I stopped being surprised. We onboarded F&B brands onto CloudKitchens infrastructure — eventually more than a hundred, across three cities — and every single one, without exception, couldn't tell me the same fact twice. The photo on their website didn't match the one on the delivery app. Three people at the same company gave three different answers for the tagline, all approximately right. These weren't incompetent operators. It was something else.

A brand's actual truth lives scattered across a dozen systems nobody owns end to end — the CRM has the customer data, the support tickets have the honest complaints, the ad platform has the creative that actually converted, buried under everything that didn't. So every time someone has to brief a campaign, they're not briefing from truth. They're briefing from whatever fragment happened to be in front of them that week.

That's why the pitch takes six weeks. From the outside it looks like process — discovery, workshops, stakeholder interviews. What you actually get is a very expensive restatement of facts someone in the building already knew, just never written down anywhere that outlived the person who knew them. That's not strategy, it's reconstruction, and you pay agency rates for it every time because nothing persists between engagements.

Putting AI into that workflow made it worse before it made it better. A model can write a hundred ad variants before you finish your coffee — capability stopped being the constraint. But it can't know, unprompted, what's actually true about the brand. Feed it a stale price or an outdated positioning line and it executes the mistake with exactly the same fluent confidence it would've used to execute the right one. A junior account exec might pause. The model won't. Speed without correction is just a faster way to be wrong at scale.

So what I ended up building wasn't a better campaign — it was a place for the truth to live. A structured record pulled from the actual systems, kept current, instead of reconstructed from memory every time someone needs a brief. It's the same problem I learned in kitchens, wearing a different uniform: the businesses that compound are the ones where somebody finally wrote the truth down in one place, properly, once.

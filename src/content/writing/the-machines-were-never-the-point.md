I was ten the first time I opened a computer case on purpose.

Not to fix anything. I wanted to see what was actually in there — the motherboard, the sound card, the ribbon of cables that made the thing think. I was learning C++ at NIIT around the same time, which at ten meant I understood maybe half of what I was typing and loved all of it anyway. Two years later I sat the Microsoft Certified Systems Engineer exam — a certification built for adults running corporate networks, not twelve-year-olds — and passed it.

At home, this made me the person everyone called when Windows 95 did something inexplicable. Friends, family, their friends — the machine would freeze or lie or simply refuse, and I was the kid who found it fascinating rather than frustrating. Other people saw a computer breaking. I saw a system I hadn't fully mapped yet, which is a different feeling entirely.

### The quiet years

That instinct didn't become a career, at least not directly. The next two decades went into finance and operations — audit at KPMG, then running restaurant estates, then building cloud kitchen infrastructure, then sitting on both sides of the table in capital raises. Good work, real work, but not the work of taking a system apart to see what was inside it.

The instinct wasn't gone. It just didn't have a machine worth opening.

### Then it did

When I started actually working with AI — not reading about it, using it — the same feeling came back immediately, and it came back hard. I went through models and tools the way I used to go through a PC case: compulsively, trying to find the edge of what each one could actually do versus what it claimed to do. Most people I knew were using one tool for one job. I wanted to understand the whole stack.

That compulsion is the reason ClarityHQ exists at all. Somewhere in the middle of testing everything against everything, the question stopped being *what can this do* and became *what can I build with it* — and a run of real client work gave me the chance to find out.

### What actually got built

Four of those builds are live, in production, run by real businesses today, and I've written each one up properly:

- **[E-Commerce Infrastructure](/work/travaholic)** — a full commerce backbone for [Travaholic](https://travaholic.in), live in two days instead of the usual two months.
- **[Booking & Property Management](/work/travaholic-stays)** — a direct-booking system for [Travaholic Stays](https://travaholicstays.com), replacing Airbnb, WhatsApp, and a spreadsheet with one platform.
- **[Info & Enquiry Platform](/work/thefeelingco)** — a complete digital operation for [The Feeling Co.](https://thefeelingco.online), a boutique wedding-planning business, running without a single external subscription.
- **[Marketplace Platform](/work/india-contemporary)** — a two-sided marketplace for [India Contemporary](https://indiacontemporary.net), connecting Indian contemporary artists directly to European collectors.

Four different businesses, four different problems, and the same underlying instinct behind all of them: open the thing up, understand every part of it, then put it back together better than it was.

### The part that didn't change

Here is what twenty years and one technological revolution didn't touch: the machine was never the point. At ten, the interesting part wasn't the motherboard — it was understanding it well enough that it stopped being a black box. Now, in an industry saturated with AI tools, the interesting part still isn't the model. It's understanding it well enough to know exactly where to point it, and where not to.

That's the whole thesis, really, just wearing different hardware. AI has made capability cheap. What was scarce when I was ten — someone who actually understood the system instead of just using it — is still scarce now. I just get to work on a much bigger machine.
